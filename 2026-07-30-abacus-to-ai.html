From Abacus to AI: What 1970's Space Rescue Teaches Us About Compute

Published: 2026-07-30 · Category: Industry Perspective

In 2035, three astronauts face a crisis in deep space. Their only help comes from 1970 — a team of Chinese space pioneers armed with nothing but paper, chalk, and abacuses. The story is fiction. The lesson is not.

A recent Chinese sci-fi film, When the Stars Shine, begins with a premise that sounds like a thought experiment: what if astronauts stranded in 2035 received a distress signal — and the only people who could help were space engineers from 1970?

No AI. No cloud computing. No real-time telemetry. Just rooms filled with old instruments, paper blueprints, and — abacuses. Six thousand of them. The data they produced, calculated by hand, brought the astronauts home.

The film is fiction. But the underlying history is real. In the 1970s, when China was locked out of global space collaboration and denied access to foreign technology, its first-generation "compute power" was human brains plus manual calculation tools. No GPUs. No clusters. Just people, working with whatever they had, to solve problems that looked impossible.

Tools change. Autonomy doesn't.

Today, the conversation has shifted to GPUs, cloud clusters, and frontier models. But the underlying question is the same: who controls the tools you depend on? If your entire stack runs on someone else's model, and that model gets restricted, your system stops. If your supply chain is a single API endpoint, and that endpoint gets cut off, your product breaks.

The engineers of 1970 had no alternative: they couldn't switch to a different supercomputer, because none existed. They built their own compute from scratch. Today's developers have a different challenge — not scarcity, but lock-in. The tools exist. The question is whether you can switch when you need to.

AI gets stronger. Decision-making stays human.

The film makes a quiet but powerful point: across 65 years of technological change, the thing that actually solved the crisis was not the most advanced machine. It was the creativity of the people using whatever tools they had.

AI can compute faster than any human. It can write code, translate documents, drive agents. But it cannot ask the right question. It cannot decide which direction to go. It cannot create meaning from data. Those remain human capabilities — and they always will.

For developers, the practical lesson is clear: choose tools that don't lock you in. Our platform provides 20 models through one API endpoint. Most have open-source versions. If one model gets restricted, developers can switch immediately or deploy locally. We've run daily public benchmarks for 24 consecutive days — 60 tests per day, zero failures. In a world where tools change faster than ever, the ability to switch tools without breaking your workflow is the most important infrastructure decision you can make.

From abacuses to AI, the tools have changed. The principle hasn't: the people who control their own tools control their own future.
