Why Huang and Musk Are Praising Chinese AI — And What's Really at Stake

Published: 2026-07-29 · Category: Industry Analysis

While the US keeps studying how to block Chinese AI, Elon Musk and Jensen Huang both publicly said on the same day: the more you try to contain it, the harder it is to stop. But their praise isn't just about China. It's about a battle happening inside the US AI industry right now.

Musk: China is very likely to become a global AI leader.

Huang: China is destined to produce excellent AI. Open-source models like DeepSeek and Kimi should be used by American companies. The US should keep learning from China.

Coming from two of the most powerful figures in American tech, on the same day, these comments sparked immediate attention. But if we only see the praise, we miss what's really driving it.

1. What they see: containment can't stop open-source

Both leaders recognize a reality that's becoming impossible to ignore: Chinese AI can no longer be stopped by export controls alone. The assumption used to be that cutting off advanced chips would prevent China from building competitive models. But Chinese models are not only catching up faster — they're cheaper, more open, and increasingly adopted by developers and enterprises worldwide.

Open-source models, by definition, have weights that can be downloaded locally. Enterprises can deploy, modify, and use them without being permanently tied to any single model provider. Once these models spread globally, no export ban can pull them back.

2. But they're not "taking China's side" — they're fighting a battle inside the US

The real context for Huang and Musk's comments is a fierce debate inside the American AI industry: open-source versus closed-source.

Closed-source companies like Anthropic have been consistently arguing that frontier AI carries serious risks — cyberattacks, biological threats — and that open-source models, once released, can't be controlled by their creators. In their view, open-source should be treated as a higher-risk category, subject to stricter safety evaluations and market access rules.

These risks are real. As AI enters the agent era, the danger has shifted from "AI saying something wrong" to "AI doing something wrong."

But safety isn't just a technical issue. It can also become an industrial barrier. Whoever defines what counts as dangerous, and whoever sets the standards for evaluation and market access, holds the cards in the AI era. If these rules are shaped primarily by a handful of leading closed-source companies, today's technical advantage can harden into tomorrow's regulatory advantage.

Safety should be a guardrail. But handled poorly, it can become a moat.

The real debate isn't whether we need safety. It's whether safety should be achieved by locking models inside a few companies, or through public evaluation, peer review, access controls, and legal accountability.

This is why neither Huang nor Musk wants the rules written entirely by a few closed-source players. Musk has xAI — he has no interest in letting OpenAI and Anthropic write their current lead into tomorrow's law. And he values China not just for models, but for power, manufacturing, supply chains, and robotics — because the AI competition of the future won't just happen on benchmark leaderboards. It will happen in power plants, factories, and robot assembly lines.

Huang's calculation is even more direct. He's not betting on which model wins. He's betting that whoever wins, they'll need more compute. The more open-source models exist, the cheaper they become, the more enterprises deploy AI — and the more GPUs, servers, and data centers the world needs. His first post on X was in support of open-weight models. His real fear isn't that China will catch up — it's that the US will strangle its own open-source ecosystem in the name of containing China.

3. What this means for China: don't celebrate, and don't panic

On the surface, two American tech titans are praising Chinese AI. Beneath the surface, the American AI industry is realigning around open-source, closed-source, and who gets to write the rules.

For China, neither complacency nor anxiety is the right response. Being told you might lead doesn't mean you already do. Being told containment is hard doesn't mean it won't create difficulties. What China actually needs to do: keep strengthening open-source models. Build sovereign compute capacity. Get AI into enterprises, factories, vehicles, and robots. And establish its own systems for data security, model safety, and agent governance.

Models can be open-source. Core technology must not be held hostage. Ecosystems can be open. Security capability must stay in your own hands.
