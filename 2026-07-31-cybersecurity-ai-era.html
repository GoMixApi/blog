Why Wall Street Is Betting on Cybersecurity as AI Gets Stronger

Published: 2026-07-31 · Category: Industry Analysis

Bank of America just raised price targets for Cloudflare, Palo Alto Networks, and CrowdStrike. The reasoning is straightforward: the more AI is deployed, the more data enterprises generate, the more complex their systems become — and the more attack surfaces they create.

On the surface, this is a story about stock prices. But underneath, it reflects something much larger: capital markets are starting to reprice cybersecurity for the agent era.

The real risk in the agent era: not what AI says, but what AI does

In the near future, every employee will work alongside several — possibly dozens — of AI agents. These agents will access databases, read enterprise files, operate computers, call APIs, and directly participate in procurement, finance, customer service, and R&D. Enterprises won't just be managing hundreds of human employees. They'll be managing hundreds or thousands of "digital employees" with system-level access.

A chatbot making a mistake might say something wrong. An agent with permissions making a mistake could delete the wrong data, send the wrong file, trigger the wrong system, or leak proprietary information. The biggest risk in the agent era is no longer AI saying the wrong thing. It's AI with permissions doing the wrong thing.

And it's not just external threats. Hackers are already using AI to automate vulnerability discovery, generate attack vectors, and probe systems at machine speed. Attacks are becoming more automated. Defenses must follow.

The harder problem: internal data security

For many enterprises, the more difficult challenge is internal data governance. Different roles — executives, finance, sales, R&D, general staff — should never see the same data. Agents acting on behalf of different people must inherit different permissions. A finance agent can view financial reports but should not casually access R&D code. A sales agent can use customer data but should not export the entire customer database on a single instruction.

Deploying AI in the enterprise cannot mean plugging in a large model, granting broad permissions, and letting it run. Who can see what data? Which systems can an agent call? Which operations require confirmation? Can every step be logged and traced? Can anomalous behavior trigger an immediate stop? These are not nice-to-have features. They are the foundational requirements for enterprise AI. Without them, more capable AI means more risk, not less.

The future of cyber defense: agent vs. agent

The future of cybersecurity will shift from human attacker vs. human defender — to attack agent vs. defense agent. Both sides will automate. The side that automates faster will have the advantage.

What Bank of America is betting on is not just a few cybersecurity companies. It's a long-term structural trend: the more AI proliferates, the more enterprises must spend on security. In the past, many companies treated security as a cost center — something to minimize as long as nothing went wrong. In the agent era, security becomes the prerequisite for whether an enterprise dares to use AI at all.

Going forward, enterprises won't just ask whether an AI is capable. They'll ask: once it has permissions, can my data leak? Can every action be traced? Can a mistake be stopped and contained? Reliable enterprise AI isn't just about getting things done. It's about not getting things wrong.
