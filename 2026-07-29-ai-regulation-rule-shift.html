When Safety Becomes a Gatekeeper: AI Competition Is Shifting From Models to Rules

Published: 2026-07-29 · Category: Industry Analysis

The AI competition is entering a new phase. It's no longer just about who builds the strongest model. It's about who defines what "safe" means — and who gets to enforce it.

Demis Hassabis, Nobel laureate and CEO of Google DeepMind, published a detailed proposal in 2024 for AI governance. He suggested that the United States should establish a dedicated agency to evaluate frontier AI models. Before any major model is released, it would undergo mandatory safety testing. Only models that pass would be allowed to enter the US market.

What would they test? Not whether the model can hold a conversation. They'd test whether it can help launch cyberattacks, create biological risks, bypass safety restrictions, deceive humans, or evade control. As AI capabilities grow — especially in the agent era, where models can operate computers, call tools, and execute real-world tasks — safety becomes critical. A chatbot making a mistake might say something wrong. An agent making a mistake might actually do something wrong.

But the real question isn't whether we need safety testing. It's: who gets to define what "safe" means?

Once safety evaluations are tied to market access, they stop being just technical standards. They become industrial barriers. What qualifies as a frontier model? What capabilities are considered dangerous? What tests are sufficient to pass? If these standards are set by a handful of US institutions and leading AI companies, then Chinese AI developers aren't just facing one more exam. The people drawing the lines and grading the papers might also be your competitors.

Open-Source Models: The Innovation Engine Caught in the Crossfire

This framework is particularly worth watching for Chinese open-source models — which happen to be China's most competitive AI strategy today.

Models like DeepSeek, Qwen, GLM, and others are expanding global influence through open weights, low costs, and developer ecosystems. But the mechanism Hassabis proposes is built around how closed-source models are released. A closed-source company can gate access, test internally, then gradually open up. An open-source model, once its weights are published, can be downloaded, copied, modified, and deployed globally — it's nearly impossible for any single institution to control the release timeline.

This isn't just about whether open-source developers have to submit paperwork. A system designed around the controlled-release logic of closed-source models may be structurally incompatible with open-source development. And the path that faces the highest compliance costs and barriers to entry may well be the one where China is strongest.

When leading companies participate in shaping regulatory rules, they may be solving real safety problems. They may also be turning high compliance costs into their own competitive moat. Large corporations can afford testing, evaluation, and legal compliance. Smaller companies, open-source communities, and newcomers may not.

What This Means for Developers: Three Things You Can Do Now

At GoMixAPI, we serve developers and businesses across Southeast Asia. The question they ask isn't "who made the rules?" It's "will my API suddenly break because of regulatory changes?", "where does my data go?", "can my provider give me a DPA to sign?"

In the face of global AI regulatory uncertainty, here are three things every developer and business should do right now:

1. Make sure your API provider is a real, regulated entity

We chose to operate through a Hong Kong-licensed company, not a vague "offshore entity" designed to evade oversight. This means our customers get a legally recognized Data Processing Agreement (DPA) that their procurement and legal teams can actually sign off on. When regulations tighten, a provider with a clear jurisdiction is far more trustworthy than one whose legal status is unclear.

2. Choose models that are open and locally deployable, to reduce supply risk

Most of the 20 models on our platform have open-source versions available. This means even if a specific model becomes restricted due to regulatory changes, our customers can still find alternatives through us, or deploy them in their own environments. This is exactly the point we made in our analysis of the OpenAI-Hugging Face security incident: when a security event happens, you need models that can run locally, without external filters, without anyone's permission.

3. Trust public data, not marketing claims

We've published daily benchmark data on all 20 models for 23 consecutive days, all publicly available on GitHub. Latency, success rate, output length — every number is verifiable. We do this because we believe that in an era of regulatory uncertainty, transparent data is more convincing than any marketing promise. Developers don't need to believe what we say. They can see what we do.

Real Global AI Governance Needs More Voices at the Table

At the recent World AI Conference, China proposed establishing a global AI cooperation organization, emphasizing openness, inclusivity, and shared benefits. We agree with this direction. True global AI governance cannot be decided by a handful of countries and corporations. More nations, and the open-source community, deserve a seat at the table.

In the past, the US used chip and computing power restrictions to limit Chinese AI. In the future, safety standards and market access rules may become the new mechanism for turning technical advantage into regulatory advantage.

What will determine AI competitiveness going forward is not just how strong your model is. It's also who gets to define what "safe" means — and who gets to write the rules of the market.

For developers, choosing an API provider isn't just a technical decision anymore. It's also a choice about how to navigate the regulatory uncertainty that lies ahead.
